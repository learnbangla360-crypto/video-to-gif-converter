A video to GIF converter picks a short stretch of a video, grabs still frames from it and stitches them into one looping animated image. This free online video to GIF converter does it entirely in your browser. You choose the start and end, the width, the frame rate and the number of colors, and it builds a standard GIF you can drop into chats, emails, documentation and social posts. There's no signup, no watermark and no upload, so private clips stay on your device. The whole point is a small, smooth, shareable loop in under a minute.

Step-by-step guide to making a GIF from a video

1. Add your video

Drag a video onto the drop area, tap it to choose a file, or paste a copied video file. MP4 with H.264 and WebM play in nearly every browser, and MOV often works too. If the page can't play your file, convert it to MP4 first. A video preview appears as soon as it loads.

2. Pick the moment

Scrub the preview to the exact second you want to begin, then press "Use current time" under Start. Do the same for End. You can also type seconds directly, such as 2.4. If the clip is long and you want a rough cut first, the Video Trimmer makes it easy to shorten the source file.

Aim for 3 to 6 seconds. A GIF is a loop, so the best ones have a clear start and finish, and a small action that repeats well.

3. Choose size and frame rate

Width has the biggest effect on file size. 480 pixels is a good default for chats and web pages, 320 suits small previews, and 640 or 800 are for when detail matters more than weight. The tool never enlarges a video, so a 360-pixel-wide source stays at 360 even if you pick 640.

For frame rate, 10 per second keeps files light and looks fine for most screen captures and reactions. Use 15 for sports or fast panning. Go to 20 only for short clips.

4. Set colors, dithering and looping

256 colors gives the best look. Dropping to 128, 64 or 32 shrinks the file, which can be worth it for flat graphics and screen recordings with few colors. Dithering adds a fine pattern that makes gradients look smoother but raises the file size. Looping forever is the default.

5. Convert and download

Press Convert to GIF. A progress bar shows each frame as it's captured and encoded, and you can cancel at any time. When it's done, the GIF plays in the result area with its size, frame count and length shown underneath. Press Download GIF to save it. If it's heavier than you'd like, go back, lower the width or frame rate, and convert again.

Expert tips and common mistakes

Tips for a smaller, sharper GIF

  • Shorter is almost always better. Cutting a clip from 6 seconds to 3 halves the frames and roughly halves the file.
  • Crop the subject before you export. Large empty areas and wide black bars cost pixels. If the source has them, trim the video first with the Compress Video tool or crop your frames.
  • Screen recordings love fewer colors. Flat interface screens usually look fine at 64 colors and shrink noticeably.
  • Photos and faces need 256 colors. Add dithering only if you see banding in smooth areas.
  • Pick a loop-friendly moment. End on a pose close to where you began, so the jump back to the start isn't jarring.
  • Keep text readable. Small captions blur at 320 pixels. Use 480 or more if people need to read them.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Converting a full 30-second clip at 800 pixels and wondering why it's huge.
  • Expecting sound. GIF has none.
  • Playing the video while it's converting. The preview locks during conversion for this reason.
  • Setting 20 frames per second for a static screen recording where 8 would look identical.
  • Using a GIF where a short MP4 would do. For long clips or smooth photos, video is far smaller.

Troubleshooting

  • The video won't load. Your browser may not support its codec, which is common with HEVC (H.265). Convert it to MP4 (H.264) or WebM, then try again.
  • The GIF is too large. Lower the width, use 10 frames per second, trim the range and try 128 or 64 colors. Each change cuts size on its own.
  • The colors look grainy or banded. Use 256 colors and try the dithering option. Banding in gradients is a limit of the GIF format.
  • The conversion is slow. Large widths and many frames take longer on phones and older laptops. Try 320 or 480 pixels and a shorter range.
  • The error says there are too many frames. The limit is 450 frames. Shorten the clip or lower the frame rate.
  • The GIF doesn't loop. Check that Loop is set to Forever. Some apps show only the first frame of a GIF, so test it in a browser tab.
  • The preview jumps or stalls. Large videos need time to buffer. Wait a moment after loading before pressing Use current time.
  • The file is over 500 MB. Shorten it first with the Video Trimmer, then convert the shorter file.

Glossary

GIF
Graphics Interchange Format, an image format that can hold several frames and play them as an animation. Wikipedia covers its history.
Frame rate
How many still frames play each second. Higher rates look smoother and make larger files.
Color palette
The set of colors a GIF is allowed to use. One GIF frame can hold at most 256.
Dithering
A pattern of tiny dots that fakes in-between colors, which smooths gradients when the palette is limited.
Codec
The method a video file uses to compress picture data. H.264 plays almost everywhere. The MDN video guide explains browser support.
Loop count
How many times a GIF repeats. Forever is the common setting for reactions and demos.

Frequently asked questions

Yes. It's free with no trial, no watermark and no account. You can convert as many clips as you like. The limits are technical: video files up to 500 MB, clips up to 30 seconds and 450 frames. Your own device does the work, so a slower phone or laptop will simply take longer on big clips.

What video formats can I convert to GIF?

Any video your browser can play. In practice that means MP4 with H.264, WebM and often MOV. Some files, such as HEVC (H.265) videos, won't play in every browser. If you see an error, convert the file to MP4 (H.264) or WebM first. For MP4 files in particular, you can also try the MP4 to GIF Converter.

Why is my GIF file so big?

GIF compresses poorly compared with video, and size grows with width, frame rate and length. A wide, long, smooth clip can reach many megabytes. Lower the width to 320 or 480 pixels, drop to 10 frames per second, trim a second or two, and use fewer colors. Turn dithering off, since it adds detail the compressor can't squeeze.

What frame rate is best for a GIF?

Ten to fifteen frames per second suits most clips. Ten keeps files small and still looks smooth for screen recordings and reactions. Fifteen helps with fast action. Going to 20 makes motion fluid but roughly doubles the frame count compared with 10, so the file grows quickly. Five to eight works for slow demos.

Does a GIF keep the sound from my video?

No. GIF is a silent format and has no audio track, so the sound is dropped. If you need audio, share the original video or a short MP4 instead. If you only need the sound, a separate video to MP3 tool is the right choice. The GIF made here contains only the picture.

How long can the GIF be?

This tool accepts clips up to 30 seconds and up to 450 frames, whichever you reach first. For most uses, 3 to 6 seconds is plenty and gives a file you can actually share. If your clip is longer, set a start and end time, or cut the video first with the Video Trimmer.

Will the GIF lose quality compared to the video?

Yes, to some degree. A GIF frame can only use up to 256 colors, so photos and gradients may look grainier than the source. This tool builds one shared palette from the clip. Dithering can smooth gradients at the cost of a larger file, and a smaller width hides some of the color loss.

Can I use the converter on my phone or offline?

Yes to both, with limits. The page works on current iPhone and Android browsers with large tap targets. Once it has fully loaded, the conversion needs no connection, because nothing goes to a server. Opening the page offline for the first time only works if your browser has it cached, so load it once while you're online.
